Can You Sue Someone for Leaving a Fake Review?

GrowGigs Team

Platform reporting isn't the only option for a genuinely fake review, but legal action is a bigger step than most businesses realize. Here's what's actually involved.

What you'd actually be suing for

A fake review lawsuit is almost always a defamation claim: a false statement of fact, published to a third party, that damages your reputation. This distinction matters because defamation protects against false statements of fact, not opinions. "Worst service I've ever had" is an opinion. "This business stole money from my credit card" is a factual claim, and if it's false and damaging, that's a different category entirely.

What you'd need to prove

Generally: the statement is false, it was presented as fact, it caused real harm, and the person knew or should have known it was false, or acted recklessly regarding its truth. Proving a reviewer was never actually a customer is often the easiest part. Proving the statement caused measurable harm, and that it clears the legal bar for defamation rather than just being unflattering, is where most cases get complicated.

Why most businesses don't go this route

Litigation costs money and time, usually far more of both than the review itself cost you in lost business. You'd also typically need to identify the reviewer first, which can mean a separate legal process, a subpoena to the platform, before you even file the actual claim. For a single review from an anonymous account, the economics rarely make sense.

When it's actually worth considering

A pattern of coordinated fake reviews, not just one, reviews that cross into clearly false factual claims with provable harm rather than just an unfair rating, or a case where you can readily identify the reviewer, a known competitor, a disgruntled ex-employee posting under a real account, are the scenarios where legal action is more realistic. Even then, most attorneys will tell you to exhaust platform reporting first since it's free and often faster.

What usually makes more sense first

Flagging the review through the platform's own policy-violation process costs nothing and resolves a real share of clearly fake reviews without legal involvement. A well-reasoned public response to a review that's opinion-based rather than a clear factual lie protects your reputation to anyone reading it, often more effectively than a drawn-out legal fight over a single review.

What makes a potential case stronger or weaker

A strong case usually has a few things in common: the statement is specific and checkable (a named incident, a claimed transaction, a specific accusation) rather than vague criticism, you have records that directly contradict it, and you can show the statement actually cost you business, a canceled contract, a documented drop tied to that specific review, not just a general sense of harm. A weak case tends to rest on "this made us look bad" without anything concrete behind either the falsity or the damage. Attorneys who handle this area will usually tell you within one conversation which category your situation falls into.

What this doesn't cover

None of this is legal advice for your specific situation, defamation law varies by jurisdiction, and the line between protected opinion and an actionable false statement isn't always obvious from the outside. If you're seriously considering legal action over a review, that's a conversation for an attorney who can look at the actual statement, the actual harm, and the actual law that applies where you operate.

The honest bottom line

Suing over a review is legally possible in the right circumstances, but it's a real legal process with real costs, not a quick fix. For the vast majority of fake or unfair reviews, platform reporting and a thoughtful response get you further, faster, and for free.
