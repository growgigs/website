What Happens If a White-Label Partner and Reselling Agency Disagree Over a Payment?

GrowGigs Team

An invoice comes in for more cases than the agency expected to be billed for, or a revenue-share calculation doesn't match what the agency tracked on its end. This is an operational dispute between two businesses, entirely separate from the end client, and how it gets resolved depends heavily on what the partnership agreement actually says, which is exactly why that document matters more than either side's memory of the conversation.

Why this is different from a client-facing dispute

The end client typically never sees or needs to know about a billing disagreement between the agency and its white-label provider, this is a vendor relationship issue that should resolve entirely behind the scenes. The agency's job is keeping it that way, continuing to deliver reliably to the client while the underlying payment question gets worked out separately, rather than letting friction with a vendor leak into the client relationship.

Where disputes most commonly originate

  • Case-count discrepancies. The provider's system shows more billable cases worked than the agency's own tracking reflects, often from a mismatch in what counts as "worked" versus "resolved."
  • Revenue-share calculation differences. Especially with tiered or volume-based splits, a disagreement over which tier applied in a given month.
  • Scope creep on what's included. Work the provider considers outside the flat fee that the agency assumed was bundled in.

What the contract should already settle

A well-drafted agreement, the kind covered in the earlier post on SLAs and contract guarantees, defines exactly what counts as a billable unit, how disputes over that count get resolved, and what documentation each side is expected to provide (case logs, timestamps, correspondence) to support a billing claim. When this is specified upfront, a dispute becomes a matter of checking records against a defined standard rather than an argument about intent.

The practical first step regardless of what the contract says

Before escalating, both sides pulling their own case logs and comparing them line by line usually resolves the disagreement faster than any formal process, since most billing disputes come down to one specific discrepancy (a case logged differently, a tier threshold crossed by a small margin) rather than a fundamental disagreement about the arrangement itself. A specific, itemized comparison moves the conversation from "something's wrong" to "here's exactly where the numbers diverge."

When it doesn't resolve at that level

If a line-by-line review doesn't settle it, most partnership agreements specify a next step, a defined escalation contact, a mediation clause, or in rarer cases a hold on future work pending resolution. Continuing to pay disputed amounts in full while a smaller, specific discrepancy gets resolved is usually more sustainable for the relationship than withholding an entire invoice over one disputed line item, assuming the rest of the invoice isn't in question.

What this means for choosing a partner upfront

A provider that's vague or resistant when asked directly how billing disputes get handled, before any money has actually changed hands, is a signal worth weighing heavily during the vetting process the same way you'd weigh their SLA terms or track record. The clearest sign of a sustainable partnership is a provider willing to spell out exactly what happens when the numbers don't match, not just how the normal case works.

Why keeping detailed records protects both sides

An agency that keeps its own running log of cases sent, dates, and expected billing treatment, rather than relying entirely on the provider's reporting, is in a far stronger position if a dispute ever comes up, since it has an independent record to compare against rather than having to reconstruct the history after the fact. This isn't about distrust of the provider, it's the same basic bookkeeping discipline an agency would want for any vendor relationship involving recurring, variable billing.

What a repeated pattern of disputes actually signals

A single billing disagreement, resolved cleanly through a records comparison, is a normal part of an operational relationship and not a reason for alarm. A pattern of recurring disputes over the same kind of discrepancy, though, suggests either a structural gap in how the contract defines billable work or a deeper reliability issue with the provider's own tracking systems, and that's worth addressing directly rather than resolving the same argument every billing cycle.
