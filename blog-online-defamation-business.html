What Counts as Online Defamation Against a Business?

GrowGigs Team

"Defamation" gets thrown around a lot for content people simply don't like, but it's a specific legal category with real requirements. Here's what actually qualifies, and what's just an opinion you disagree with.

The core requirement: a false statement of fact

Defamation requires a false statement presented as fact, not an opinion, that damages your reputation and was published to someone other than you. "This restaurant gave me food poisoning" is a factual claim, either true or false. "I think this restaurant is overpriced" is an opinion, protected speech even if you strongly disagree with it.

Opinion vs. fact isn't always obvious

Courts and platforms look at how a reasonable reader would interpret the statement. "In my opinion, they're scamming customers" can still be treated as a factual accusation dressed up as opinion, adding "I think" doesn't automatically protect a false factual claim. This is genuinely one of the harder lines to draw, and it's worth getting specific legal input on if you're considering formal action.

What typically qualifies

  • False claims about specific events. Accusing you of something that didn't happen, a specific incident, a specific failure, that can be shown to be untrue.
  • False claims about credentials or legal status. Falsely stating you're unlicensed, uninsured, or under investigation.
  • Fabricated quotes or events attributed to your business. Content presenting something as having happened that simply didn't.

What typically doesn't qualify

  • Genuine negative opinions. "Worst experience I've ever had" is an opinion, however harsh.
  • Accurate but unflattering facts. True statements aren't defamatory just because they're damaging.
  • Hyperbole a reasonable reader wouldn't take literally. Obvious exaggeration is generally treated differently than a literal factual claim.

What to do if you believe it's genuine defamation

Document the content (screenshots, URL, date), and consider what evidence you have that the statement is false. Many platforms have a defamation or legal-content reporting process separate from standard content moderation, since it often requires a different kind of review. For content clearly rising to the level of defamation, especially content causing real business harm, consulting an attorney about your options, including a formal legal request to the platform or host, is worth doing alongside any platform report.

The honest bottom line

Not every unfair or damaging statement is defamation, and treating every harsh review as a legal matter usually goes nowhere. The cases worth pursuing are the ones with a genuinely false, specific, factual claim behind them.
