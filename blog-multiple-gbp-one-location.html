Does Having Multiple Google Business Profiles for One Location Hurt You?

GrowGigs Team

Search your own business name and two listings show up for the same address, same phone number, slightly different names. Neither one is a rebrand or an acquisition. Somehow, over the years, your location just ended up with two profiles, and you're not entirely sure why.

How a single location ends up with two profiles

This is a different problem from the one covered in cleaning up duplicate listings after a merger or rebrand, where the duplication has a clear origin. A single, never-changed location usually ends up duplicated through an accident: a staff member or agency created a second profile not realizing one already existed, a data aggregator or directory auto-generated a listing that later got independently claimed and verified, or an old profile from a previous owner never got properly transferred when the business changed hands.

Yes, it actively hurts you

Two profiles for one location split your signals rather than doubling them. Reviews, posts, and engagement get divided across two listings instead of concentrating on one, which weakens the exact prominence signals Google uses to decide who shows up in the map pack. Worse, Google's own systems are actively looking for this pattern and can suppress or suspend either or both profiles once detected, treating it as a guideline violation rather than an innocent oversight.

How to find out if it's actually happening to you

  • Search your business name and address directly in Google Maps and in regular search, not just your own saved listing, to see what a stranger would actually find.
  • Check Google's own duplicate-flagging tools inside Business Profile Manager, which sometimes surfaces a second listing at the same address automatically.
  • Search variant spellings of your business name, abbreviations, old DBAs, slightly different formatting, since duplicates rarely use the exact same name string.

This is different from a fake duplicate someone else created

If the second listing wasn't created by you or a past employee but by a competitor or malicious actor, the fix is a report against someone else's listing rather than a self-merge, since you have no admin access to remove it yourself.

The merge process, when both are legitimately yours

Google's official path is to claim and verify both profiles, then request a merge through Business Profile support, rather than simply deleting one and hoping the other absorbs its history. Deleting a profile outright can lose review history and verification status attached to it, while a proper merge is more likely to consolidate that value into the surviving listing.

What happens to reviews during a merge

Reviews generally do not transfer automatically in a merge, which is the hard part businesses are often unprepared for. The reviews on the profile that gets absorbed are typically lost rather than combined with the surviving listing's reviews, so if one of the two profiles holds the bulk of your review history, that's the one you want to establish as primary going into the request, not an afterthought decided after the merge already happened.

Watch for this after a category or service-area change

Businesses sometimes inadvertently create a second profile while trying to add a new service area or correct a category, using the "Add a business" flow instead of editing the existing listing. If you're making structural changes to an existing profile, always confirm you're editing the original rather than starting a fresh listing from scratch.

Preventing it from happening again

Limit who has admin access to create or claim listings on your business's behalf, and keep a clear internal record of exactly one canonical profile URL that every team member, agency, or contractor is instructed to use. Most duplicate-profile situations trace back to more than one person independently trying to "set up" the same business without checking what already existed.
