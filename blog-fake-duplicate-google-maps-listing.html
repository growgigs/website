Reporting a Duplicate or Fake Google Maps Listing for a Competitor's Location

GrowGigs Team

Fake and duplicate listings on Google Maps are a real, recognized problem, not just a competitor playing dirty in your head. Here's how the reporting process actually works, and what actually qualifies.

What actually counts as a violation

Google's guidelines are specific about what's not allowed: a business creating multiple listings for the same physical location, a listing for a business that doesn't have a real, physical presence at the address shown, a listing using a business name stuffed with keywords or services it doesn't actually offer, or a listing that impersonates or closely mimics another real business. A listing you simply dislike, or that's outcompeting you in local search, isn't a violation on its own. The report process is for policy violations, not competitive frustration.

How to flag it

Google Maps has a "Suggest an edit" option on any listing, which includes a path to report it as closed, a duplicate, or inappropriate. For a more detailed violation report, Google Business Profile's own support channels and the Business Profile help community accept more specific complaints with documentation, useful when the violation isn't obvious from the listing alone and needs explanation.

What makes a report more likely to be acted on

Specificity and evidence. "This listing is spam" without detail is far weaker than a report that names the exact issue, two listings for the identical street address, a business name with extra unrelated keywords tacked on, a photo or address that doesn't match any real operating location, and includes screenshots or other documentation supporting the claim. Google receives a high volume of these reports, and vague ones are easy to deprioritize.

What usually doesn't qualify

A competitor ranking above you isn't inherently suspicious, even if it's frustrating. A legitimately multi-location business with several real listings isn't duplicating, each physical location is entitled to its own profile. And a business operating from a legitimate shared or flexible workspace address isn't automatically fake just because it isn't a traditional storefront. The reporting process is for listings that violate specific policies, not for listings that are simply doing better than yours.

What to expect after reporting

Google reviews reports against its own guidelines and decides independently, there's no guaranteed outcome or fixed turnaround. Reports with clear, well-documented violations tend to get resolved; vague or borderline ones often don't move at all. This is consistent with how most platform moderation works, specific and well-evidenced reports fare better than general complaints across the board.

Why this matters beyond just annoyance

A fake or duplicate listing isn't just clutter, it can actively hurt real businesses. Two listings for one location can split reviews and visibility between them, confusing both customers and Google's own ranking signals. A listing claiming services or a location that doesn't actually exist can mislead customers directly, showing up in searches it has no legitimate claim to. Reporting a genuine violation isn't just about your own ranking, it also keeps local search results accurate for everyone searching that category.

Keep a record of what you report

Screenshot the listing before and after you file a report, note the date you submitted it, and save whatever reference or ticket number you're given. If you need to follow up or escalate, having a clear record of exactly what was reported and when makes that conversation far more straightforward than trying to reconstruct it from memory weeks later.

The bottom line

If you're looking at a listing that genuinely violates Google's duplicate, spam, or misrepresentation policies, the reporting path exists and does work for real violations. If the actual issue is that a competitor is simply ranking well, that's a local SEO question, not a policy-violation one, and worth approaching differently.
