What Is a Google Business Profile "Suggest an Edit" and Should You Worry About It?

GrowGigs Team

You open your Google Business Profile one day and something's different, the hours changed, the category shifted, or the address has a typo you didn't add. Nobody on your team touched it. This is almost always "Suggest an Edit" at work, a feature most business owners don't know exists until it changes something of theirs.

What Suggest an Edit actually is

Any Google user viewing a business listing on Maps or Search can submit a proposed correction, hours, phone number, address, category, accessibility details, and more. Google built this specifically to keep listing data current when a business itself hasn't updated anything, relying on crowd-sourced corrections rather than only the business's own input.

How a suggested edit actually goes live

Not every suggestion applies instantly. Minor edits that match patterns Google's systems already trust, especially ones that align with data from other sources, can apply automatically. Others queue for review, either by Google's own systems or, in some cases, by you as the verified owner, depending on the field and how established your profile is. This is why some changes seem to appear overnight while others don't show up at all.

Why this matters more than it sounds like

A wrong phone number or incorrect hours directly costs you customers, someone calls a dead line or shows up when you're closed. A changed category can hurt your visibility for the exact searches that used to find you, tied directly into the same ranking signals covered in how to choose the right Google Business Profile categories. This isn't a cosmetic issue, it's a real local SEO and customer-experience risk.

Legitimate correction vs. malicious edit

Most suggested edits are genuinely well-intentioned, a customer noticing your hours changed and trying to help. Occasionally, though, a suggested edit is deliberately malicious, a competitor submitting a wrong phone number or address to redirect your customers. Google's review process catches a meaningful share of bad-faith edits, but not all of them, which is why periodically checking your own listing against what you know to be accurate matters.

How to catch and fix a bad edit

  • Check your profile regularly. A quick glance every so often catches drift before it costs you business.
  • Use the edit history where available. Google's Business Profile dashboard shows recent changes in some cases, letting you see what changed and roughly when.
  • Correct it directly through your dashboard. As the verified owner, you can edit the field back, which generally carries more weight than an anonymous suggestion.
  • Report a pattern of bad-faith edits. If you suspect a competitor is behind repeated malicious suggestions, Google has a path to report that specifically, separate from a one-off correction.

Keeping this from being a recurring problem

A profile that's actively maintained, correct categories, current hours, a filled-out description, consistent NAP data matching the rest of the web, is less of a target for this kind of drift in the first place, and easier to catch quickly when something does slip through.

Which fields are most commonly affected

Hours and phone number see the most suggested edits in practice, since those are the details a customer is most likely to notice are wrong in the moment, standing outside a closed storefront, or dialing a disconnected line. Address and category changes are less frequent but carry more consequence when they do go through, since both feed directly into which searches your listing even appears for.

What happens if you're not actively watching

A profile that goes unchecked for months can accumulate several small, unreviewed changes that compound: a slightly wrong closing time here, a secondary category swapped out there. None of these look dramatic individually, which is exactly why they tend to go unnoticed, but together they can meaningfully erode how your listing performs and how accurately it represents your business to someone seeing it for the first time.
