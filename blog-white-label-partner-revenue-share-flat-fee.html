How White-Label Partners Get Paid: Revenue Share vs. Flat Fee

GrowGigs Team

Before signing on as a white-label partner, it's worth understanding exactly how the money actually works, since "white-label" alone doesn't tell you much about the compensation structure underneath it.

Revenue share

Under a revenue-share model, the agency marks up the underlying provider's price and keeps the difference, or splits an agreed percentage of what the end client pays. This scales naturally, more clients and higher-value cases mean more margin, with no separate negotiation needed per deal. It also means the agency's margin is tied directly to what the underlying provider actually charges, which is worth knowing upfront rather than discovering later.

Flat fee or wholesale pricing

Here, the agency pays a fixed wholesale rate per unit of work, per review removed, per case handled, and sets its own retail price to the client independently. This gives the agency more control over margin and client-facing pricing, but it also means the agency carries more of the pricing-strategy work itself, deciding what's actually a fair markup for its own market and client base.

Why this distinction matters before you sign anything

A revenue-share arrangement is simpler to start with, less pricing decision-making, but caps how much control the agency has over its own margin. A wholesale arrangement takes more setup, you have to decide your own retail pricing, but gives more room to build a margin that reflects your own positioning and client relationships. Neither is inherently better, they suit different agency sizes and sales approaches.

Questions worth asking before committing to either model

What happens to the fee when a removal attempt doesn't succeed, is it refunded, credited, or lost entirely? Is pricing locked in for a period, or can the underlying provider change wholesale rates without much notice? Who handles client communication if something goes wrong, the agency, or does the white-label provider step in directly? The answers to these matter more than which model, revenue-share or flat-fee, sounds better on paper.

How this connects to pay-per-result pricing specifically

For a service like review removal, where the underlying model is pay-per-result rather than a flat retainer, the compensation structure between agency and provider usually mirrors that: the agency typically only owes the wholesale cost once a review actually comes down, not for attempts that don't succeed. That alignment matters, it means the agency isn't carrying financial risk on cases that don't resolve.

Which model fits which kind of agency

A newer agency still figuring out its market positioning and pricing confidence often does better starting with revenue share, it removes one variable (what to charge wholesale-adjacent clients) while the agency builds its sales process. An established agency with a clear sense of its own client value and pricing power tends to prefer wholesale, since it can capture a larger margin by setting retail pricing that reflects its own brand and relationships rather than a pre-set split.

Don't skip the exit terms

Beyond day-to-day compensation, it's worth understanding what happens if the partnership ends, whether that's your choice or the provider's. Do existing client cases in progress get completed under the old terms? Is there a transition period, or does access end immediately? These questions rarely come up during the excitement of signing a new partnership, but they matter a great deal if circumstances change later.

The practical takeaway

Before agreeing to any white-label arrangement, get the actual compensation mechanics in writing: whether it's revenue-share or wholesale, what happens on an unsuccessful case, and how pricing changes get communicated. The label "white-label partner" describes the branding arrangement, not the financial one, and those are two separate things worth understanding clearly before you start selling.
