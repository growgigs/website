Can a Former Employee's Negative Glassdoor Review Be Removed?

GrowGigs Team

A negative review from someone who left on bad terms feels personal, and it's tempting to assume it should come down just because the relationship ended badly. Here's what actually determines whether it qualifies.

A bad parting isn't a policy violation on its own

Glassdoor's guidelines don't distinguish between a current employee's review and a former one, what matters is whether the content itself violates specific guidelines, not how or why the employment relationship ended. A former employee who left on bad terms and writes an honest, if critical, account of their experience is doing exactly what the platform is for, even if it's uncomfortable to read.

What actually does matter

Content that crosses into harassment, discriminatory language, personal attacks on a named individual, or disclosure of confidential company information is reportable regardless of whether the author is a current or former employee. The timing or circumstances of their departure don't factor into whether the review meets these criteria, the content does.

When the timing itself is relevant

There is one place departure timing matters: if a review describes conditions or events from a timeframe that doesn't line up with when that person actually worked there, or if it references specifics, a role, a location, a manager, that don't match any record you have, that's a legitimate basis to question the review's accuracy. Not because they're a former employee, but because the facts themselves don't check out.

Why retaliatory-sounding reviews still usually stay up

It's common for a review following a difficult termination or resignation to read as retaliatory in tone. That alone doesn't make it fake or against guidelines. Genuine employees, including ones who left angry, are allowed to describe genuine experiences in a genuine, even harsh, way. The same "critical but accurate reviews stay up" principle that governs other review platforms applies here too.

What to actually do about one

If the review violates a specific guideline, harassment, confidential disclosure, personal attack, flag it through Glassdoor's Employer Center citing that specific violation. If it's a genuine, if harsh, account of a real experience, a thoughtful public response, acknowledging what's fair and addressing what's specific, generally does more for how your company is perceived than pursuing a removal that likely won't succeed.

A useful way to separate your reaction from the actual case

Before flagging, it helps to write down specifically what guideline you believe the review violates, in a sentence, independent of how the departure felt. "This is unfair because they were let go for performance issues" isn't a guideline violation. "This review discloses specific confidential financial figures" is. If you can't state the violation without referencing the circumstances of their exit, that's a sign the review itself may not actually qualify, however justified the frustration feels.

Multiple reviews following one departure

Occasionally a single departure is followed by more than one negative review, sometimes from the same person using different language, sometimes coordinated with others. A genuine pattern of coordinated, inauthentic activity is itself a guideline violation worth reporting, distinct from any single review's content. That's a different case to build than disputing one review on its own merits, and it's worth treating it as such if you suspect it's happening.

The bottom line

"They were a former employee" and "they left on bad terms" aren't grounds for removal by themselves. What matters is whether the content itself violates a specific guideline, the same standard applied to every review regardless of when or how the employment ended.
