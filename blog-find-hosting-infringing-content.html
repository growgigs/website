How to Find Who's Hosting Infringing Content

GrowGigs Team

A takedown request only works if it reaches the party who can actually act on it. When infringing content lives on an unfamiliar site rather than a major platform, the first job is figuring out who's actually behind it.

Start with the site itself

Check for a Terms of Service, Privacy Policy, "Contact Us," or "Report Abuse" page, many sites, even small ones, list a designated DMCA agent or abuse contact as a legal requirement. This is the fastest path when it exists, and it's worth checking before anything else.

Run a WHOIS lookup on the domain

A WHOIS lookup shows the domain's registrar and, when not hidden behind privacy protection, the registrant's contact details. Many domain owners use privacy services that mask this information, so don't be surprised if WHOIS alone doesn't get you a name, it often gets you a registrar instead, which is still useful.

Identify the hosting provider

Separately from who registered the domain, you need to know who hosts the actual content, the server it's running on. Tools that check a site's IP address and hosting provider (a simple web search for "who is hosting [domain]" surfaces several free tools) will point you to the hosting company, which usually has its own abuse-reporting process independent of the site owner.

Why the hosting provider matters even when the site owner won't respond

If a site owner ignores your request or has no visible contact information, the hosting provider is your next lever. Most hosts have an abuse policy and will act on legitimate copyright or trademark complaints against content on their servers, since hosting clearly infringing material creates liability exposure for them too.

Check for a CDN in front of the actual host

Some sites run behind a content delivery network (Cloudflare is the most common), which can mask the true hosting provider in a basic IP lookup. If your hosting lookup returns a CDN rather than what looks like a real host, that CDN typically has its own abuse process, and some will pass along or verify complaints even when they don't host the actual content.

Registrar-level options as a last resort

If neither the site nor the host responds, and the content is clearly infringing, the domain registrar is a further point of contact, though registrars are generally more conservative about acting on registrant-level complaints than hosts are about content complaints. This route takes longer and works best when paired with clear documentation of the infringement.

Document everything as you go

Screenshot the infringing content, the WHOIS record, and the hosting lookup results as you find them, sites change registration details and hosting providers, and having timestamped records strengthens any complaint you eventually file.
