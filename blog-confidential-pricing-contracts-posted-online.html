What to Do If Confidential Pricing or Contracts Get Posted Online

GrowGigs Team

A leaked pricing sheet, a vendor contract, or internal negotiation terms showing up on a public site is a different kind of problem from a bad review or a fake account. There's no "report this review" button for it, and the right response depends heavily on how the document got out.

First, figure out how it actually got out

The response differs a lot depending on the source: a disgruntled former employee, a vendor or partner who shared it improperly, a security breach, or simply a document that was never properly restricted and got indexed by search engines. Knowing which one you're dealing with shapes everything that follows, a security incident needs different handling than a one-off leak from a single bad actor.

Check whether the document is actually your copyrighted work

If the leaked material is something you wrote, a contract template, a pricing document, internal materials, it's your original work and covered by copyright the moment you created it. That makes a DMCA takedown a legitimate option against whoever is hosting it publicly, even though the content is confidential business information rather than creative work in the traditional sense.

If a contract specifically prohibits disclosure

Where the leaked material came from a counterparty bound by an NDA or a confidentiality clause in a signed contract, that's a breach-of-contract matter separate from and often stronger than a copyright claim. A direct, documented request citing the specific clause, sent to the party who leaked it, is usually the first and fastest step, before considering what if anything to pursue against whoever is hosting the public copy.

Finding who's actually hosting it

If the leak shows up on a site you don't control, start the same way you would for any other removal request: identify the actual hosting provider rather than just the domain's public-facing owner, the same lookup process used for a copied article or any other infringing content.

When it involves trade secrets specifically

Pricing structures, vendor terms, and proprietary processes can qualify as trade secrets under law if you've taken reasonable steps to keep them confidential, a separate legal category from copyright or trademark. Trade secret misappropriation claims are a stronger tool than a standard takedown request when the leak came from someone with a legal duty to protect the information, but they typically require legal counsel to pursue properly, this isn't a self-service platform report the way a fake review is.

Document everything immediately

Screenshot the leaked content with the URL and timestamp before you do anything else, leaked documents on obscure sites can disappear or move just as easily as they appeared, and you want a clear record of what was exposed and where, both for any takedown request and for understanding the scope of what's actually out there.

What this usually isn't

Unlike a defamatory statement or a fake review, a leaked internal document is typically accurate, the problem isn't that it's false, it's that it was never supposed to be public. That distinction matters because it rules out defamation as the legal basis and points you toward copyright, contract, or trade secret law instead, whichever actually fits how the leak happened.

If the leak came from inside your own organization

A current or former employee posting internal pricing or contracts, whether out of frustration, in a dispute, or simply carelessly, raises a separate question from a third-party leak: what obligations did that person have, and do you have signed confidentiality agreements covering what they shared. If they do, that agreement is often your most direct lever, a documented breach of a signed NDA is more straightforward to act on than trying to prove trade secret status from scratch. Review what agreements are actually in place before deciding which legal basis to pursue.

Why acting quickly matters more here than with most content issues

Pricing and contract terms lose their competitive value the longer they stay visible, a competitor only needs to see a leaked rate sheet once to use it. Unlike a review or a piece of defamatory content, where the harm is reputational and somewhat ongoing regardless of exact timing, a pricing leak's damage is largely done the moment a competitor or customer actually sees it. That's worth factoring into how urgently you pursue removal versus other priorities.
