Can a Trustpilot Review Be Removed If the Same Person Posted It From Multiple Accounts?

GrowGigs Team

A single customer creating two or three Trustpilot accounts to post the same complaint, or variations of it, multiple times is a distinct abuse pattern from a fake-name reviewer or a one-off conflict of interest. It inflates the apparent volume of a complaint, makes an isolated incident look like a pattern, and it's a violation Trustpilot takes seriously once it's demonstrated with real evidence.

Why this differs from ordinary duplicate reviews

Trustpilot's guidelines already address straightforward duplicates, the same reviewer posting the same review twice under one account, which is a simpler and more mechanical fix. Multiple-account abuse is harder to prove because it requires showing that accounts which LOOK unrelated, different usernames, different stated details, are in fact controlled by the same person, which takes more than just pointing at similar wording.

What actually counts as evidence here

  • Near-identical review text across accounts. Not just a shared complaint topic, but matching phrasing, sentence structure, or specific details that would be an unlikely coincidence between two unrelated customers.
  • Matching transaction or interaction details. References to the same order number, same staff interaction, same date and circumstances, described across what are supposedly different customers' experiences.
  • A known identity behind multiple accounts. Cases where the business can independently identify the person (an employee dispute, a known complainant who's stated outright they created multiple accounts, or a traceable pattern from their own records).
  • Timing patterns. Multiple accounts posting near-identical content within a short window of each other, which is harder to explain as independent customers than reviews spread naturally over time.

Why this is harder than the fake-name scenario

Unlike a fake-name reviewer, where the dispute is usually about whether the person transacted with the business at all, multiple-account abuse often starts from a real complaint, just duplicated for amplification. Trustpilot has to weigh removing content from someone who may have had a genuine (if exaggerated through repetition) experience, which means the evidence bar for proving it's genuinely the same person across accounts matters more here than in a simpler conflict-of-interest case.

How to present this to Trustpilot

Lay out the specific accounts involved, the matching content or details between them, and why this pattern is implausible as independent customer behavior, rather than asserting multi-account abuse as a conclusion without the supporting comparison. A side-by-side excerpt of the matching language from each account, flagged clearly, makes the pattern easy for a reviewer on Trustpilot's side to evaluate quickly.

What doesn't qualify as this violation

Multiple genuinely different customers independently having similar complaints about a real, recurring issue (a consistently slow shipping process, for instance) isn't multi-account abuse, it's multiple people reporting the same real problem, which Trustpilot won't remove on a multi-account theory just because the complaints resemble each other. The distinction is specifically about one person operating several accounts, not several people having similar experiences.

Where this overlaps with incentivized or coordinated review patterns

Multi-account abuse sometimes shows up alongside coordinated or incentivized review activity, where a single actor behind multiple accounts is also tied to a broader campaign rather than an isolated grievance. If you're seeing both patterns together, present them as related but distinct violations rather than folding everything into one undifferentiated complaint, since Trustpilot evaluates each guideline violation on its own terms.

A realistic expectation on outcome

Strong, specific evidence of multi-account abuse is one of the more persuasive categories once documented clearly, but it's also one of the more labor-intensive to build, since it requires actually comparing accounts side by side rather than citing a general suspicion. Businesses that invest the time to lay out the comparison clearly tend to get a faster, more confident response than those that simply flag "this looks like the same person" without showing the specific match.
