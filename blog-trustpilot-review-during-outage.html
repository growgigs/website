How Does Trustpilot Handle a Review Left During a Service Outage or System Failure?

GrowGigs Team

A payment processor goes down for six hours, a booking system crashes during a product launch, a server outage takes your site offline for an afternoon, and a frustrated customer who hit that exact window leaves a scathing one-star review describing your business as unreliable. The review reflects something real that happened, but it also reflects a one-time circumstance rather than how your business actually operates day to day.

Trustpilot has no specific "outage" policy category

Unlike grounds such as hate speech or conflict of interest, there's no dedicated Trustpilot policy addressing reviews written during a documented outage or system failure, which means this isn't a removable category on its own, however unfair the timing feels. A review describing a genuine experience, even one caused by a temporary technical failure outside your control, generally meets Trustpilot's "genuine experience" standard the same as any other authentic complaint.

Why this differs from an off-topic or outdated-information case

A review is removable for being off-topic when it doesn't actually describe an experience with your business at all, and removable for citing information that's since changed when the underlying fact is simply no longer accurate. An outage review usually fails both tests for removal, it's clearly about your business, and the outage genuinely happened as described, which is exactly why this case is harder than either of those.

What actually might make an outage review removable

  • The review misrepresents the cause, falsely blaming your business for a failure that was actually caused by a third-party vendor or an external infrastructure provider, when the review states this as established fact rather than speculation.
  • The review includes content unrelated to the outage itself, hate speech, threats, or a fabricated additional complaint layered onto an otherwise legitimate outage-related frustration.
  • The reviewer wasn't actually affected, someone posting about an outage they didn't personally experience, which would fail the genuine-experience standard on its own separate grounds.

Your most effective path is usually a public response, not a report

Respond publicly with the specific facts, when the outage happened, what caused it, what you did to fix it, and what changes you've made since, since this response often does more for your reputation than pursuing a removal that likely won't succeed. A specific, factual response demonstrates accountability and context to every future reader, which matters more here than it does for most negative reviews, since outage complaints are inherently time-stamped to a real, verifiable event.

Build a standing response template before the next outage happens

Any business running on third-party infrastructure, payment processors, booking platforms, hosting providers, will eventually face this exact situation again, so draft a response template in advance covering acknowledgment, brief explanation, and resolution steps, ready to adapt quickly rather than drafted from scratch mid-crisis. Speed matters here, a prompt, specific response to an outage-related review reads very differently to other customers than one that arrives weeks later.

Consider proactively addressing a known outage before reviews pile up

If you know an outage affected many customers at once, a brief public statement, a pinned social post, an email to affected customers, acknowledging it before reviews start arriving can reduce how many customers feel compelled to vent through a review in the first place. This doesn't prevent every review, but it genuinely reduces the volume, since many customers who already feel heard through direct acknowledgment have less motivation to post publicly about the same incident.

One outage-driven review rarely does lasting damage on its own

A single review tied to a clearly time-stamped, one-time incident reads very differently to a prospective customer than a pattern of complaints spread across months, especially once your factual response sits right alongside it. Resist the urge to treat one outage review as an emergency requiring aggressive removal attempts, since the honest, measured path, a good public response plus genuine process improvements, tends to hold up better over time than a removal fight you're unlikely to actually win.
