What's the Difference Between a Verified Review and an Unverified One?

GrowGigs Team

A small "verified" badge next to a review looks like a minor UI detail, but it represents a specific claim the platform is making, that this particular reviewer took some confirmable action tied to your business, a purchase, a visit, an account tied to a transaction. An unverified review makes no such claim either way, and understanding exactly what that badge does and doesn't promise changes how much weight you, and your customers, should give it.

What verification actually confirms, platform by platform

On Trustpilot, verification typically means the reviewer was invited through a confirmed transaction, an order number or receipt tied to an actual purchase. On Google, there's no equivalent formal verification tier, every review is simply attributed to a Google account, which is a much lower bar than a confirmed transaction record. The specific mechanics differ enough between platforms that "verified" on one doesn't mean the same thing as "verified" on another.

What verification does not confirm

  • It doesn't confirm the review's content is accurate, a verified reviewer can still misrepresent what happened during a genuine transaction.
  • It doesn't confirm the reviewer is being honest about intent, a genuine customer can still be posting in bad faith, say, after a dispute unrelated to the actual service quality.
  • It doesn't prevent a reviewer from being connected to a competitor in a way that creates a conflict of interest, even with a real, verified transaction on record.

Why this distinction matters for how you respond

A verified negative review generally deserves a direct, solution-focused public response, since the reviewer genuinely did transact with you and the underlying complaint is worth taking seriously on its face. An unverified review calling out specifics you can't match to any account on file is worth investigating before assuming it's fabricated, but it's also a more legitimate candidate for a policy-based report if it turns out the reviewer was never actually a customer at all.

Customers read verification as a trust signal, often more than it genuinely deserves

Prospective customers tend to weight a verified badge heavily when deciding how much to trust a review, sometimes more heavily than the actual substance of what the review itself says. This cuts both ways for a business, a handful of verified five-star reviews can carry outsized influence, but so can a single verified negative one, which is part of why responding thoughtfully and promptly to verified complaints specifically tends to matter considerably more for conversion than responding to unverified ones does.

Don't assume unverified automatically means fake

A genuine customer can easily end up leaving an unverified review, they bought in person with cash, they used a platform with no transaction-linking feature, or they simply weren't logged into the account the purchase was tied to. Treat "unverified" as a neutral fact about the platform's data, not as evidence of dishonesty on its own, the actual signals of a fake review are a separate, more specific set of red flags worth evaluating independently.

Think about which platforms actually offer verification before relying on it

If building verified social proof matters to your business, it's worth knowing which platforms your industry's customers actually use that even offer a meaningful verification tier in the first place, since pushing customers toward a platform with weak or no verification won't accomplish much on that front. A platform with genuine transaction-linked verification tends to carry more credibility with cautious buyers specifically because the badge actually means something concrete, not just a label the platform applies loosely to any account, and that genuinely matters more in categories where trust is already hard-won.
