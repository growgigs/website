What Happens to a Business's Reviews When Ownership Changes?

GrowGigs Team

Buy a business with an existing Google listing and you inherit everything attached to it, including every review left under the previous owner. That can be a windfall or a liability depending on what's there, and the options for dealing with it are narrower than most new owners expect.

The listing doesn't reset on a sale

Google, Yelp, and similar platforms tie reviews to the business listing itself, not to whoever currently owns it. Buying the business, the location, or the brand name doesn't create a fresh listing unless you actively set one up as a new entity. If you keep the same name, address, and phone number, the old reviews (good and bad) stay exactly where they are, visible to anyone searching for you today.

What you can update

  • Who manages the listing. You can claim or transfer ownership of the Google Business Profile itself so you control responses, photos, and hours going forward.
  • How you respond going forward. You can post owner responses to old reviews explaining new management, new policies, or changes made since the review was written. This doesn't remove anything, but it gives context to anyone reading.
  • New reviews under new management. Nothing stops you from building a wave of current, accurate reviews that reflect the business as it operates now, which naturally pushes older reviews further down the list over time.

What you generally can't do

You can't remove a review just because it predates your ownership, platforms don't treat "that happened before I owned it" as grounds for removal. The reviews describe real experiences with that business at that location, and from the platform's perspective, the business is a continuous entity regardless of who's running it. The same removal standards apply as always: the review has to violate actual content policy to come down, not just be inconvenient for a new owner.

When starting a new listing actually makes sense

If you're genuinely rebranding, new business name, new concept, different service entirely, it can be appropriate to close the old listing and open a new one rather than carry the old reputation forward under new branding. This is a legitimate path, but it needs to reflect a real change in the business, not just be a way to shed bad reviews while operating the same business under the same name and address. Platforms do watch for that pattern.

Due diligence before you buy

If you're considering a purchase, pull the full review history before closing, not just the star rating. A 4.2-star average can hide a pattern worth knowing about: recent complaints about something structural (billing, a specific manager, a recurring service issue) that you'd be inheriting along with the business. That's worth factoring into the deal itself, not just something to handle after the fact.

If the previous owner already disputed something

Ask whether any reviews were previously reported or disputed and what the outcome was. If a review was already reviewed by the platform and left standing, filing the same dispute again under new ownership generally won't change the result, platforms look at the content, not who's asking. What can change the equation is new information the previous owner didn't have or didn't use, so it's worth a fresh look rather than assuming a prior "no" is final, but go in knowing the bar hasn't moved just because the business changed hands.

Setting expectations with staff and customers

If you're keeping the same name and location, regular customers and even current reviewers may not know ownership changed at all. A short note in your Google Business Profile description or a pinned owner response explaining the transition gives context without requesting anything from the platform itself, and it's the kind of signal that helps new reviews reflect the business as it is now rather than carrying forward assumptions about how it used to be run.
