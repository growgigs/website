Does Closing and Reopening Under a New Name Reset Your Reviews?

GrowGigs Team

Same location, same team, same service, just a new name and a fresh coat of paint. It's a tempting idea when a review history has gotten ugly: close the old listing, open a new one, start clean with a blank slate online. The reality is more complicated, and in some cases this approach can backfire badly.

This is different from an actual ownership change

A genuine sale to a new owner is a related but separate scenario with its own considerations. This one is different: the same owner, same operation, rebranding under a new name without any real change in who's running it. Platforms evaluate these very differently.

Why a cosmetic rebrand doesn't reliably work

Google and other platforms look at more than just the business name when deciding whether a new listing is genuinely new or a continuation of an existing one: the address, the phone number, the category, and sometimes the underlying business registration all factor in. A listing at the same address with the same phone number, just a different name, reads as the same business under a new label, not a fresh start, and can be merged back with the old listing's history or flagged as a duplicate.

What actually counts as a legitimate new listing

A genuine change, new ownership, a materially different concept or service, a real relocation, is more likely to be treated as a new business by platforms. "Same business, new sign" generally isn't, regardless of how much the brand identity changes. The test isn't how different the name looks, it's whether the underlying operation actually changed in a way a customer or regulator would recognize as a real transition, not just a cosmetic one.

The risk of trying anyway

Businesses that create a second listing at the same address specifically to shed bad reviews, while the original listing stays active or barely changes, risk the new listing being flagged as a duplicate or spam listing. That can mean losing the new listing entirely, which is a worse outcome than the review history you were trying to escape.

What this doesn't mean

None of this means rebranding is off the table, businesses rename and refresh their identity for real reasons all the time. It means a rebrand doesn't function as a review-reset mechanism, and shouldn't be the reason you do it. If the actual goal is addressing a damaged review history, that's a removal and reputation problem to solve directly, not something a new name quietly fixes on its own.

What a real rebrand should do instead

If you're rebranding for genuine business reasons, new ownership, a real pivot in what you offer, expansion into a new concept, carry the same profile forward and update it to reflect the change rather than trying to start a second one at the same address. Update the name, description, and category to match the new identity, and use owner responses to give context to older reviews if the change is significant enough to warrant it. This keeps your review history, trust signals, and local SEO standing intact while still reflecting who you are now.

The honest path if your reviews have gotten bad

Reviews don't expire or quietly age out on their own either, so waiting it out isn't a real strategy, and neither is a cosmetic rename dressed up as a fresh start. Addressing the reviews that actually violate policy, and building a stronger base of recent, genuine reviews under your real identity, is the path that holds up.
