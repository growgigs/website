What to Do When a Competitor Copies Your Website Content

GrowGigs Team

Finding your own website copy, word for word, on a competitor's site is frustrating and can hurt your search rankings if Google can't easily tell which version is the original. It's also a clear-cut copyright issue with a defined process for fixing it.

Confirm it's actually copied, not just similar

Two businesses in the same industry will naturally use similar language, service descriptions and industry terminology overlap all the time. What you're looking for is substantial word-for-word duplication, whole sentences or paragraphs lifted directly, not general topical similarity. That distinction matters because a DMCA claim needs to identify specific copied content, not just a shared theme.

Document the original and the copy

Screenshot both pages side by side, note the URLs, and if possible pull up an archived version of your page (via the Wayback Machine or your own site's version history) that predates the copy. Establishing that your content existed first is central to a copyright claim.

File a DMCA takedown with the host

Once you've confirmed the copying and have your documentation, a DMCA takedown notice goes to the hosting provider (found via a hosting lookup if the site's own contact info doesn't have a copyright agent listed). The notice needs to identify the original work, its location, the infringing location, and a statement made under penalty of perjury that you're the rights holder or authorized to act for them.

Google's own duplicate content reporting

Separately from a DMCA request to the host, Google has its own process for reporting content that's been scraped or copied in a way that affects search results. This won't necessarily remove the content from the competitor's site, but it can address how it's being indexed and ranked.

Why speed matters here

Search engines sometimes struggle to identify which version of duplicate content is the original, especially if the copy gets indexed and picked up quickly. Reporting the issue and, where relevant, having your archived proof ready sooner rather than later reduces the risk of ranking confusion in the meantime.

What if it's a direct competitor rather than an anonymous site

A direct message or a formal cease-and-desist letter (from an attorney, if the situation warrants it) can sometimes resolve this faster than a platform process, a competitor with an actual business reputation to protect is often more motivated to fix it quietly than an anonymous scraper site would be. This isn't a substitute for the DMCA process, it's an additional option worth considering alongside it.
