What's the Difference Between Reporting a Review and Disputing It?

GrowGigs Team

These two words get used almost interchangeably by business owners, but platforms often treat them as genuinely different actions. Knowing which one you're actually doing matters.

Reporting: flagging a policy violation

Reporting a review means telling the platform it violates a specific content guideline, it's fake, abusive, discloses private information, isn't based on a genuine experience. This is the process covered throughout this blog for actual removal, it's evaluated against the platform's own written policies, and it's the mechanism that can result in a review actually coming down.

Disputing: contesting the facts within a response

Disputing, in the sense most business owners mean it, usually refers to publicly responding to a review and contesting what it claims, correcting a factual error, explaining your side, pushing back on an inaccurate detail. This doesn't go through any policy-violation evaluation process, it's just a public reply visible alongside the original review. The review stays up either way, your response simply sits next to it.

Why conflating the two leads to disappointment

A business owner who writes a strongly worded public response "disputing" a review, and expects that to function like a report, is often surprised when the review doesn't come down. That's not how disputing works, it doesn't trigger any evaluation against platform policy. Only an actual report does that. Confusing the two means businesses sometimes do the wrong one, writing an aggressive public rebuttal when what they actually wanted was removal, or vice versa.

When each one is the right tool

Use reporting when you believe a review genuinely violates a specific guideline and you want it evaluated for removal. Use disputing, a public response, when the review is likely to stay up regardless, it's genuine, even if you disagree with parts of it, and your goal is making sure anyone reading has your side of the story too. These aren't mutually exclusive, you can report a review and, if it doesn't get removed, still respond to it publicly afterward.

A third option worth naming: doing both

For a review that's borderline, where you're not fully certain it qualifies for removal, filing a report while also preparing a thoughtful public response covers both outcomes. If the report succeeds, the response becomes moot. If it doesn't, you already have a considered reply ready rather than reacting after the fact.

Why the terminology confusion is so common

Part of the issue is that everyday language around disagreements, "I'm disputing this," "I'm contesting that," naturally carries a sense of formal process, like a credit card chargeback or a legal dispute. Review platforms don't have an equivalent formal "dispute" mechanism separate from reporting and responding, so the word gets borrowed and applied loosely, which is exactly where the confusion comes from.

What this looks like in practice

A business gets a review claiming a refund was never issued, when records show it was. Reporting that review as containing a false statement of fact is the path that could actually get it removed, since it's a specific, checkable claim. Responding publicly with "Our records show this refund was processed on [date]" is the path that puts the correction in front of anyone reading, regardless of whether the review itself ever comes down. Many businesses in this exact situation do both, and that's usually the right call.

The bottom line

Reporting is the mechanism that can actually get a review removed, evaluated against specific platform policy. Disputing, in the everyday sense of publicly responding, doesn't change whether the review stays up, it just gives your side visibility next to it. Knowing which outcome you actually want determines which one is worth doing.
