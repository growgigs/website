Should Agencies Disclose to Clients That Work Is Being White-Labeled?

GrowGigs Team

A client hires an agency believing the work happens entirely in-house, when in reality a specialized white-label partner is doing the actual execution behind the scenes. This is standard practice across the industry, but whether to tell the client is a question agencies answer very differently, and the right answer depends on more than just preference.

There's no universal legal requirement, but context matters

In most jurisdictions, there's no blanket legal requirement to disclose subcontracting arrangements to a client, the agency is generally the party responsible for the deliverable regardless of who actually performs it. That said, certain contexts raise the stakes: if your own contract with the client makes specific representations about who performs the work, or if the client's industry involves regulatory requirements around vendor disclosure, silence can become a real problem rather than just an awkward one.

Check your own client contract language first

  • Does it explicitly promise in-house delivery? A specific representation that work is performed by your own team creates a real misrepresentation risk if it's actually subcontracted.
  • Does it stay silent on this point? Most standard service agreements don't address it either way, which generally leaves more room, though "more room" isn't the same as "no obligation at all."
  • Does the client's own industry require vendor disclosure? Regulated sectors, healthcare, finance, government contracting, sometimes carry specific requirements around subcontractor transparency that override your own general practice.

The trust argument for disclosure

Clients who discover white-labeling after the fact, rather than being told upfront, often react more to the lack of disclosure than to the subcontracting itself. A client who finds out later tends to ask what else wasn't disclosed, which damages the relationship more broadly than the actual practice of using a specialized partner would have on its own.

The case for staying quiet, and when it actually holds up

Agencies that don't disclose generally argue the client is paying for a managed outcome, not a specific org chart, and that disclosure adds no real value if quality and accountability remain entirely with the agency regardless of who executes. This argument holds up better when your vendor relationship has genuinely strong SLAs and consistent quality, since the agency is fully able to stand behind the work as if it were performed in-house.

A middle path many agencies actually use

Rather than a binary disclose-or-don't decision, some agencies describe their delivery model in general terms, "we work with a specialized network of partners for execution," without naming the specific vendor. This satisfies a baseline transparency expectation without exposing the particular partner relationship or undermining the agency's own brand as the client-facing point of accountability.

What happens if non-disclosure surfaces during a dispute

If a client discovers subcontracting during a quality dispute or after something goes wrong, the lack of prior disclosure itself can become part of the complaint, even when the actual work was never the real problem. Weigh this scenario when deciding your disclosure policy, not just the smoother case where everything goes fine and the question never comes up at all. A dispute is precisely the moment your policy gets tested in practice, and an agency caught improvising an explanation under pressure looks far worse than one that can simply point to a position it held consistently all along.

Build your policy before you need it, not during a crisis

Decide your agency's disclosure stance deliberately, and apply it consistently across clients, rather than improvising an answer client by client when someone happens to ask directly. A consistent policy, whatever you choose, is easier to defend and explain than an ad hoc one that looks different depending on who's asking.
