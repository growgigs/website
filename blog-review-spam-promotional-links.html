Can a Google Review Be Removed If It Contains Spam or Promotional Links?

GrowGigs Team

Every so often a review shows up that isn't really about your business at all, it's a one-star rating with a link to an unrelated product, a "make money from home" pitch, or an ad for a completely different company dropped into the text. This is a distinct, clearly defined policy violation, and it's usually one of the more straightforward reports to get acted on.

Why this is its own policy category

Google's review policies explicitly prohibit spam and fake content, and that category covers more than just fabricated reviews, it includes promotional material, repetitive posts linking to the same destination, and content whose real purpose is advertising rather than describing an experience with your business. A review built around a link or a pitch is being used as a distribution channel, not as feedback, and that's flagged separately from whether the star rating itself is fair.

What actually qualifies

  • Links to unrelated products or services. Affiliate links, crypto schemes, or ads for a different business entirely, dropped into the review text.
  • Recruitment or "work from home" pitches. Reviews that use the star-rating format purely as a vehicle to advertise an opportunity.
  • Repeated identical or near-identical posts. The same promotional text appearing across many businesses' listings is a strong spam signal on its own.

What doesn't qualify

A review that mentions a competitor by name as part of a genuine comparison ("I ended up going with X instead") isn't spam, even though it references another business, because the purpose is still describing the reviewer's own experience. The distinction Google draws is about intent and function: is the link or mention incidental to real feedback, or is the feedback incidental to the link.

How to report it

Flag the review through Google's standard reporting flow and select the spam or policy-violation category rather than a generic complaint, since the review text itself (the link, the pitch, the off-topic content) is usually enough evidence on its own. Screenshot it first in case the reviewer edits or deletes it before a decision comes back, that still happens occasionally and you'll want the original wording on record.

Why this differs from a self-promotion violation

A reviewer promoting their own unrelated business inside your review is a related but slightly different case from Yelp's self-promotion policy, which specifically targets someone using a review slot to advertise their own competing services. Google's spam policy is broader, covering any promotional or irrelevant link regardless of whose business it points to.

What to do if the report doesn't resolve it quickly

Spam reports are usually processed faster than content-dispute reports since the violation is often visible in the text itself, but if it sits unresolved past a couple of weeks, re-submitting with a clear screenshot and a short note pointing to the specific policy line can help. Avoid responding publicly to the review in the meantime, a reply draws more attention to content that's likely coming down anyway.

Why one spam review is worth watching for a pattern

A single promotional review is often an isolated bot or opportunist, but if a second one shows up with a similar structure, same kind of link, same recruitment pitch, same odd phrasing, that's worth noting as a pattern rather than treating each one as unrelated. Spam campaigns sometimes target a batch of businesses in the same area or category over a short window, and flagging that pattern in your report can help a platform's trust-and-safety team connect it to other reports they're already investigating, rather than evaluating your case in isolation.

What this means for your star rating in the meantime

A spam review with an attached one-star or five-star rating still factors into your visible average until it's removed, which is frustrating when the number doesn't reflect any real customer experience at all. There's no shortcut around this beyond getting the report resolved quickly, since trying to offset it with additional reviews or disputing the rating separately from the content creates its own policy risk and generally isn't worth it for what's typically a short-lived problem once the underlying spam review comes down.
