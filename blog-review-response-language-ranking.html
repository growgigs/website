Does Responding to Reviews in a Language Other Than English Affect Local Ranking?

GrowGigs Team

A customer leaves a review in Spanish, French, or Mandarin, and you're deciding whether to reply in English or in their language. It feels like a small formatting choice, but it touches on two separate things: how the reply reads to the person who wrote it, and whether it does anything for your Google Business Profile's visibility.

What Google has actually said about this

Google hasn't published a ranking factor tied specifically to the language a business owner replies in. The publicly documented local ranking signals are relevance, distance, and prominence, with review count, rating, and review response activity in general folding into prominence. Language of the response itself isn't called out as its own input, and no credible source outside SEO-mill content claims otherwise with real evidence.

Where language could plausibly matter indirectly

Even without a documented language-specific signal, a few indirect paths are worth being realistic about. If your Business Profile and website are heavily optimized for a specific language's search queries in a given market, a reply in that same language is consistent with the rest of your presence there, rather than ranking boost, think of it as coherence rather than a bonus point. There's also a simpler case: if a meaningful share of your customer base searches and reads reviews in a language other than English, replying in a language they can't read undercuts the actual purpose of the response, which is to show prospective customers (not Google) that you engage with feedback.

The engagement signal matters more than the language it's in

What's actually documented is that responding to reviews at all is one of several prominence-related signals, and that consistency of engagement across your review history matters more than any single reply's wording. A business that responds promptly and specifically, in whichever language fits the review, reads as more actively managed than one that responds sporadically or with generic templates, regardless of language.

A practical approach

  • Match the reviewer's language when you reasonably can. It's a better experience for the person who actually wrote the review, and for anyone reading in that same language later.
  • Don't rely on raw machine translation without a quick check. An awkward or slightly wrong translated reply can read worse than a short English one, especially for anything beyond a simple thank-you.
  • If you can't reply well in the review's language, a short, genuine English reply beats skipping it. Responding at all is the part tied to prominence, not which language you used to do it.
  • Stay consistent. A profile that responds reliably, in whatever language fits, builds the kind of engagement history that actually factors into prominence over time.

If you serve more than one language market

Businesses operating across language lines, a bilingual medical practice, a restaurant in a heavily Spanish-speaking neighborhood, a service business near a border, face this question constantly, not as a one-off. In those cases it's worth having a standing habit rather than deciding reply-by-reply: default to the reviewer's language when a team member can write it competently, keep a short list of vetted phrases for common review types if translation support is limited, and don't let the extra effort turn into skipping responses altogether. Skipping is the actual cost here, not the language mismatch.

What this doesn't change

Switching reply language on its own won't move a stalled ranking, and it's not a substitute for the things that actually affect local visibility: consistent business information across directories, review volume and recency, and completeness of your profile itself. Treat language choice as a customer-experience decision, because that's what it is, not an SEO lever to pull when rankings aren't moving.
