Can a Duplicate Review From the Same Customer Be Removed From Google?

GrowGigs Team

A customer leaves a one-star review, then a few days or weeks later, the same complaint shows up again, same story, same wording in places, sometimes from the same Google account, sometimes from a second one they also control. Unlike a fake review from a non-customer, the underlying facts here are often real, which raises a slightly different question: does one legitimate complaint get to count twice?

Why Google treats this as a policy issue at all

Google's review policies are built around one reviewer, one review, per listing, reflecting one genuine experience. A second post repeating the same complaint, regardless of how real the original experience was, violates that structure, it's not about whether the complaint is true, it's about whether the same voice gets two separate entries in your public rating. Google's spam and duplicate-content policies cover this even when there's no dishonesty about the underlying facts.

The two common versions of this scenario

  • Same account, posted twice. The reviewer edits or deletes and reposts, or somehow ends up with two live entries from one account, usually a platform glitch or a reviewer trying to re-draw attention to an unresolved issue.
  • Two different accounts, same person. The reviewer posts from a personal account, then again from a second email or business account they also control, intentionally or not registering as two separate customers.

How this differs from the multi-account Trustpilot scenario

The Trustpilot multiple-accounts post already covered a related but distinct situation: one person posting from several accounts specifically to inflate the appearance of multiple unhappy customers, often as an abuse pattern. This topic is narrower, it's the same person's single, real experience counted twice, which can happen without any intent to deceive, just an honest double-post that still violates the one-review rule either way.

What evidence actually supports a report

Side-by-side screenshots showing the near-identical wording, timing close enough to rule out two separate incidents, and, where available, any account details suggesting the same person (a shared name variant, a business account tied to a personal one you can identify) all strengthen a duplicate report. Google's own moderation can sometimes catch exact duplicates automatically, but a close paraphrase from a second account usually needs a direct report pointing out the connection.

What doesn't qualify

A customer who had two genuinely separate bad experiences, say a return visit that also went poorly, and wrote two different reviews describing two different incidents isn't duplicating anything, even if both are negative and about the same underlying service issue. The violation is about the same experience appearing twice, not about a repeat customer having more than one complaint over time.

What to do once a duplicate is confirmed removed

Only one of the two reviews typically needs to be reported, since removing the duplicate restores the single-review structure without needing to touch the original entry, assuming the original itself doesn't separately violate another policy. If the original complaint is legitimate, responding to it professionally after the duplicate comes down is usually the better move than trying to get both removed just because the situation was frustrating to deal with twice.

Why timing matters when you first notice it

The sooner a duplicate is reported after it appears, the easier it tends to be to demonstrate, the near-simultaneous posting dates and overlapping wording are usually most obvious right when the second review goes up, before either one accumulates its own separate replies or edits that start to differentiate them. Waiting weeks to act doesn't make a duplicate any less valid as a report, but it does mean gathering clean evidence takes a bit more digging.

What this means for your visible rating in the meantime

A duplicate review counts toward your star average the same as any other live review until it's removed, so one real negative experience effectively weighs twice on your public rating until the report resolves. This is a reasonable argument to include when filing the report, not because a lower rating alone proves a violation, but because it underscores exactly what the one-review-per-reviewer policy exists to prevent.
