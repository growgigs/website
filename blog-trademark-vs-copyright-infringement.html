Trademark Infringement vs. Copyright Infringement: What's the Difference

GrowGigs Team

These two get used interchangeably, but they protect completely different things and go through different removal processes. Filing the wrong one is a common reason a takedown request gets rejected on a technicality.

Copyright protects creative work

Copyright covers original creative content you made: photos, videos, written text, graphics, design work. It exists automatically the moment you create the work, no registration required, though registration strengthens your position if it ever goes to court. If someone copies your product photos, your website copy, or your video content without permission, that's a copyright issue, handled through a DMCA takedown.

Trademark protects your brand identity

Trademark covers your business name, logo, slogan, and other identifiers that distinguish your business from others in the marketplace, whether registered or established through use (common law trademark). If someone uses your business name, a confusingly similar name, or your logo in a way that could confuse customers about who they're dealing with, that's a trademark issue, a different process from copyright.

Where the confusion usually happens

  • A competitor copying your website text. That's copyright, the words themselves are the protected work.
  • A competitor using a name confusingly similar to yours. That's trademark, the issue is brand confusion, not copied text.
  • A fake account using your logo. That's trademark, the logo is a brand identifier being misused to impersonate you.
  • Someone reposting your product photos. That's copyright, the photos are the original creative work.
  • A domain name deceptively similar to yours. Usually trademark, sometimes handled through a separate domain dispute process rather than either standard takedown route.

Why the distinction matters practically

Platforms and hosts have separate reporting forms and requirements for each. A DMCA notice needs to identify the specific copyrighted work and its unauthorized use. A trademark complaint needs to establish your rights to the mark and explain the likelihood of confusion. Submitting a copyright claim for what's actually a trademark issue, or vice versa, is a common reason a report gets rejected without explanation, the reviewer is checking against the wrong criteria entirely.

Registered vs. unregistered rights

Copyright doesn't require registration to exist, though it helps for formal legal action. Trademark rights can exist through use alone in many jurisdictions (common law trademark), but a registered trademark generally gives you stronger, more clearly documented grounds when filing a complaint, since you can point to an official registration rather than arguing established use.

When you're not sure which applies

Ask what's actually being copied: your content, or your identity. Content copied word-for-word or image-for-image is a copyright matter. Someone trying to look like you, your name, your logo, your brand, is a trademark matter. Some situations genuinely involve both, and it's fine to pursue both processes when that's the case.
