How to Handle AI-Generated Fake Content About Your Business

GrowGigs Team

A fabricated photo of a dirty kitchen, a synthetic voice clip of an employee making a threat, a deepfake video clip circulating with your logo in the background, these aren't hypothetical anymore. AI-generated fake content about a business is a distinct problem from a written fake review, and the reporting path looks different too.

Why this isn't the same as a fake review

A written fake review is a text claim you can dispute on factual grounds, no matching transaction, policy violation, and so on. Fabricated media is different: the content itself is manufactured, meaning the dispute isn't about whether an experience happened, it's about whether the image, audio, or video is real at all. That's a cleaner, often stronger case once you can demonstrate it, but it requires different proof and often a different reporting channel than a standard review removal request.

Identify the specific type of fake content

  • Fabricated images. AI-generated or manipulated photos presented as real evidence, a fake health violation, a staged injury, a doctored receipt.
  • Synthetic audio. A cloned voice making statements no one at your business actually said, sometimes paired with a real phone number or employee name to add false credibility.
  • Deepfake video. Footage altered or generated to show your branding, staff, or premises in a fabricated scenario.
  • AI-written text presented as a genuine account. Large volumes of AI-generated review text are a related but distinct detection problem platforms already have systems for.

Platform policies are already catching up

Most major platforms, Google, Meta, YouTube, TikTok, now have explicit policies against synthetic or manipulated media presented as authentic, separate from their general misinformation or defamation policies. Citing the specific "manipulated media" or "synthetic content" policy category, where the platform has one, usually gets faster attention than a general content complaint, since it's a narrower and more clearly defined violation for a moderator to evaluate.

Document the fabrication itself

Where possible, gather evidence that the content is synthetic: metadata inconsistencies, reverse image search results showing the original unaltered source, or simply the fact that the scenario depicted never occurred and you can show records proving it. Screenshot the content immediately along with the URL, timestamp, and platform, the same baseline documentation that matters for any harassment or targeted content report, since fabricated media frequently escalates into that territory.

Does it use your actual branding or likeness?

If the fake content uses your real logo, business name, or an employee's actual likeness without consent, that opens an additional angle: a right-of-publicity or trademark-misuse claim on top of the content-authenticity issue. This is worth flagging specifically, since it often routes to a different, more responsive enforcement team than a general content report.

When it's defamatory, not just fake

Fabricated media that makes a false factual claim, your restaurant served contaminated food, your technician threatened a customer, isn't just inauthentic, it's potentially defamatory, and that distinction matters for how seriously a platform or a court takes the report. The same honest standard applies here as with any defamation claim: it has to assert something false and damaging, not just be embarrassing or unflattering.

Hosting and distribution, not just the platform

If the content is self-hosted on a website rather than a major platform, a direct hosting abuse report works the same way it does for any infringing content hosted elsewhere, identify the host through a WHOIS or IP lookup and file a report citing the specific violation, fabricated media presented as authentic, rather than a vague complaint.

When to involve legal counsel directly

AI-generated content that's demonstrably false, widely circulated, and causing measurable business harm, lost bookings, cancelled contracts, is exactly the kind of severe, hard-to-resolve case that benefits from a direct legal consultation rather than relying solely on platform reporting. A clear fabrication is often an easier legal case to build than a disputed but technically accurate negative review, since the falseness itself is demonstrable rather than a matter of opinion.
