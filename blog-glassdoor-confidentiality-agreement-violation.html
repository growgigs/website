Can You Remove a Glassdoor Review for Violating a Confidentiality or Non-Disparagement Agreement?

GrowGigs Team

A former employee who signed a severance agreement with a non-disparagement clause posts a detailed, critical Glassdoor review anyway. The business holds a signed contract that arguably covers exactly this situation, but a contract violation and a removable Glassdoor review are two different things, and conflating them leads to wasted effort.

Glassdoor doesn't enforce your private contracts

A non-disparagement or confidentiality agreement is a contract between you and the former employee, Glassdoor isn't a party to it and has no obligation to remove content just because it violates a private agreement you have with the reviewer. This is the single most important thing to understand before pursuing this route: a contract breach claim and Glassdoor's own content removal process run on entirely separate tracks, governed by entirely different rules and different decision-makers.

What the agreement actually gets you

  • A direct legal claim against the former employee for breach of contract, pursued through your own legal counsel, not through Glassdoor's moderation system.
  • Leverage to request voluntary removal. A former employee notified they've breached a signed agreement sometimes takes the review down themselves to avoid the legal exposure, without Glassdoor getting involved at all.
  • Potential damages, if the agreement includes a specific remedy clause for violations, though pursuing this requires weighing the legal cost against the actual harm done.

Enforceability varies more than businesses expect

Non-disparagement clauses face real legal limits in some jurisdictions, particularly when they attempt to restrict truthful statements about unsafe conditions, discrimination, or other matters increasingly carved out by law in several places as a matter of public policy. A clause broadly prohibiting "any negative statement" may not hold up as written if the review's substance touches a protected category of disclosure, so don't assume the agreement is automatically fully enforceable exactly as drafted on paper.

Get an actual legal read before sending any notice

Before contacting the former employee about a breach, have employment counsel actually review the specific agreement language against the specific review content, rather than assuming the clause clearly covers the situation just because it feels like it should. A notice sent on a shakier legal basis than the business realizes can backfire, inviting pushback or even a counterclaim rather than the quiet resolution most businesses expect.

What Glassdoor will actually act on, regardless of the contract

Independent of any private agreement, Glassdoor's own content guidelines still apply, non-genuine reviews, personal attacks, or content violating their standard policies remain reportable through the normal process covered in Glassdoor's general removal process. If the review happens to also violate both your agreement and Glassdoor's own policy, pursue the policy violation through Glassdoor directly, since that path doesn't require legal action to resolve.

The practical sequence that usually makes sense

Start with a direct, documented notice to the former employee citing the specific contract provision breached, this is often faster and cheaper than formal legal action and resolves many cases on its own. Escalate to legal counsel only if the notice is ignored and the review is causing real, demonstrable harm that justifies the cost of pursuing a breach claim.

Weigh the reputational cost of enforcement itself

Visibly and aggressively enforcing a non-disparagement clause against a former employee can itself become a reputational story, sometimes a worse one than the original review, particularly if it reads publicly as a company silencing legitimate criticism. Consider whether the review's actual content and reach justify that risk before escalating formally.

Build better agreements going forward, not just reactive ones

If this situation keeps recurring, revisit how your severance and confidentiality agreements are actually drafted, with input from employment counsel on how enforceable the specific language is in your jurisdiction. A clause that's realistic about what it can actually restrict is more useful going forward than one broad enough to feel comprehensive but too vague to hold up if it's ever actually tested in a real dispute.
